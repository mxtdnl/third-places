Definition

What Is Theory of Change?

"A rigorous yet participatory process whereby groups and stakeholders in a planning process articulate their long-term goals and identify the conditions they believe have to unfold for those goals to be met."

Taplin & Clark, 2012

The core move: start with where you want to end up, then map backwards to figure out how to get there. Every step in the chain must be justified - not hoped for, not assumed, but argued with evidence.

This separates ToC from a wish list. You are not listing activities and hoping they work. You are building a testable causal argument for why your intervention will produce the change you intend.

Core Structure

The Causal Pathway

A ToC maps a chain of outcomes from your ultimate goal down to the things you can directly do. Each link carries an assumption about how change happens - and that assumption needs evidence.

Long-Term Outcome
The ultimate change you seek. Requires deep understanding of the problem to define well.
Assumption: intermediate outcomes are sufficient to produce this
Intermediate Outcomes
The conditions that must exist for the long-term outcome to be reached.
Assumption: early outcomes will produce these conditions
Early Outcomes
The first observable changes your interventions should produce.
Assumption: these interventions will trigger these early changes
Interventions
What you actually do. The activities, programmes, and resources you deploy.

Read upward: each level is a precondition for the one above it. The assumptions between levels are where your evidence goes.

Critical Element

Assumptions

Every link in a causal chain rests on an assumption - a belief about how change works. Making assumptions explicit is what separates a Theory of Change from wishful thinking.

Example

Causal link: "If we build more public libraries in underserved areas, literacy rates will improve."

Hidden assumptions: Literacy is limited by access to books (not by teaching quality, time, or motivation). People in these areas will use the libraries. The books will be in languages people read. Library staff will be trained to support new readers.

Each of these assumptions needs evidence. Without it, the link is hope, not logic.

For every assumption, you need a rationale: the evidence or reasoning that justifies it. This is where disciplinary knowledge matters - psychology, economics, and politics each illuminate different assumptions.

Quality Assurance

Three Tests for a ToC

Any Theory of Change must pass three quality tests. When you build yours in weeks 9-14, these are the standards you will be held to.

Plausible

Does the causal logic hold? Is there evidence that each outcome leads to the next? Would a sceptic accept the chain?

Feasible

Can you actually do this? Do you have the resources, access, partnerships, and time? Is the scope realistic?

Testable

Can you measure it? Are your indicators specific enough to tell you if the ToC is working - or failing?

A ToC that fails any one of these tests needs rework. Plausibility without feasibility is academic. Feasibility without testability is faith. Notice: plausibility requires evidence about the problem - which is what description produces.

Real-World Use

Who Uses Theory of Change?

ToC is not an academic exercise. It is standard professional practice across the sectors where PEP graduates work.

UK Government
Required by the Analysis Function for policy programme design. The toolkit we draw on in this course is published by HM Treasury.
International Development
DFID, USAID, and the World Bank require ToC for funded programmes. You cannot get development funding without one.
Foundations
The Gates Foundation, Ford Foundation, and major philanthropic funders mandate ToC in grant applications.
Public Health
WHO and national health agencies use ToC to design and evaluate disease prevention and health system interventions.
Impact Investing
Social enterprises and impact funds use ToC to demonstrate that claimed social returns are causally linked to investment.
Management Consulting
Strategy firms use the same causal-logic framework under different names (logic models, results chains) for public sector clients.

Cautionary Tales

What Happens When You Skip Description

The most common failure mode in applied work is premature intervention: jumping to solutions before the problem is understood. These are real examples. Click each to see what went wrong.

PlayPumps (Sub-Saharan Africa) ▶
Roundabout-powered water pumps installed across Africa. Children play, water flows. $60M invested.

The intervention assumed children would play on the roundabouts enough to pump water. In practice, children did not play enough. Adults found the pumps exhausting to operate - far harder than the hand pumps they replaced. Maintenance was impossible locally. Many communities already had functioning water sources.

What Was Missing

Description of how communities actually accessed and used water. The intervention was designed around an appealing image, not an observed problem.

Scared Straight (United States) ▶
Programmes taking at-risk youth into prisons to deter criminal behaviour. Intuitively compelling.

Multiple randomised controlled trials showed that Scared Straight programmes increased offending among participants compared to control groups. The assumption - that fear of prison deters crime - was never tested against what actually drives youth offending (peer influence, socioeconomic conditions, trauma).

What Was Missing

Description of the causal mechanisms behind youth offending. The intervention targeted a plausible-sounding but empirically wrong lever.

One Laptop Per Child (Global) ▶
Distributed low-cost laptops to children in developing countries to improve educational outcomes.

Studies across multiple countries found minimal impact on learning outcomes. The programme assumed that access to technology was the binding constraint on education. In practice, the barriers were pedagogy, teacher training, curriculum design, and the educational content available - not the hardware.

What Was Missing

Description of what actually constrains learning in these contexts. The intervention treated the symptom (no laptops) without understanding the system.

The Connection

Why We Describe First

Theory of Change begins with a clearly defined long-term outcome. But you cannot define a good outcome without understanding the phenomenon you want to change.

If you do not understand the third place - what it is, how it works, why it declines - you cannot define what a successful intervention looks like. And if you cannot define success, you cannot map backwards from it.

Every session in the Describe phase builds your capacity to do this:

Description tells you what the phenomenon looks like empirically - its observable features and patterns.

Explanation tells you why it works the way it does - the causal mechanisms across psychology, economics, and politics.

Adjudication tells you which explanations hold up under scrutiny - so your ToC rests on the strongest evidence.

Intervention applies all of this to build a ToC that is plausible, feasible, and testable - because you earned the understanding to make it so.

The verb sequence is not arbitrary. It is the structure that prevents you from becoming the next PlayPumps.
