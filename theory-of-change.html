You have described the third place empirically. You have explained its decline through psychological, economic, and political lenses. You have adjudicated between competing accounts. Now: how do you intervene?

Theory of Change is the methodology that bridges analysis and action. It prevents the most common failure mode in applied work: premature intervention - jumping to solutions before the problem is understood.

Definition

What Is Theory of Change?

"A rigorous yet participatory process whereby groups and stakeholders in a planning process articulate their long-term goals and identify the conditions they believe have to unfold for those goals to be met."

Taplin & Clark, 2012

The core move: start with where you want to end up, then map backwards to figure out how to get there. Every step in the chain must be justified - not hoped for, not assumed, but argued with evidence.

This separates ToC from a wish list. You are not listing activities and hoping they work. You are building a testable causal argument for why your intervention will produce the change you intend.

Core Structure

The Causal Pathway

A ToC maps a chain of outcomes from your ultimate goal down to the things you can directly do. Each link carries an assumption about how change happens.

Long-Term Outcome
The ultimate change you seek. Set at or below the accountability ceiling.
Assumption: intermediate outcomes are sufficient to produce this
Intermediate Outcomes
The conditions that must exist for the long-term outcome to be reached.
Assumption: early outcomes will produce these conditions
Early Outcomes
The first observable changes your interventions should produce.
Assumption: these interventions will trigger these early changes
Interventions
What you actually do. The activities, programmes, and resources you deploy.

Read upward: each level is a precondition for the one above it. The assumptions between levels are where your evidence goes.

Starting Point

Long-Term Outcomes & the Accountability Ceiling

Your ToC starts at the top: what is the ultimate change you want to see? But not every change is within your power. The accountability ceiling marks the highest-level outcome you can credibly hold yourself responsible for.

Above the Ceiling - Beyond Your Reach
Reduced social isolation city-wide in Bodo
Improved mental health outcomes for residents
Reversal of third-place decline nationally
Accountability Ceiling
Below the Ceiling - Your Responsibility
A functioning community space used by diverse groups
Regular visitors who identify the space as their third place
Measurable increase in cross-group social interaction

Set your long-term outcome at or below the ceiling. Grand societal claims above it may be true, but you cannot be held to them, and they make your ToC untestable.

Method

Backward Mapping

From your long-term outcome, ask one question repeatedly: "What conditions must be in place for this to happen?" Each answer becomes a precondition. Keep asking until you reach things you can directly do.

Example: Bodo Stadium Third Place ▶

Long-term outcome: The stadium community space functions as a genuine third place for 500+ regular visitors within two years of opening.

What must be true? Diverse demographic groups use the space regularly. It is not captured by a single subculture or match-day crowd.

What must be true for that? The space is welcoming to non-football visitors. Programming and design signal broad community ownership.

What must be true for that? Community members were involved in design decisions. The space has identity independent of the football club.

What can you do? Run co-design workshops with diverse local groups. Create governance that includes non-club stakeholders. Design flexible space that accommodates multiple uses.

Why Backwards, Not Forwards? ▶

Forward planning starts with what you can do and hopes it leads somewhere good. Backward mapping starts with where you need to be and works out what must happen.

The difference: forward planning lets your resources dictate your ambition. Backward mapping lets your ambition expose what resources you actually need - and reveals assumptions you would otherwise never examine.

Critical Element

Assumptions & Rationales

Every link in your causal chain rests on an assumption - a belief about how change works. Making assumptions explicit is what separates a Theory of Change from wishful thinking.

Example

Causal link: "If diverse groups use the space regularly, community members will report a stronger sense of belonging."

Assumption: Repeated casual interaction across demographic lines builds social bonds - proximity alone is not sufficient, but structured-yet-informal contact is.

Rationale: Contact hypothesis research (Allport, 1954; Pettigrew & Tropp, 2006) shows intergroup contact reduces prejudice and builds affiliation under specific conditions: equal status, common goals, cooperation, and institutional support.

For every assumption, you need a rationale: the evidence or reasoning that justifies the assumption. This is where your disciplinary knowledge from the Describe, Explain, and Adjudicate phases pays off.

Measurement

Indicators

For each outcome in your pathway, you need an indicator that tells you whether change is actually happening. An indicator without specifics is not an indicator.

The Indicator Framework
How many of who / what will reach what level by when
Example Indicator ▶

Outcome: Diverse demographic groups use the space regularly.

Indicator: At least 200 unique visitors per week from at least 4 distinct age brackets and 3 neighbourhood zones use the community space at least twice monthly within 6 months of opening.

Bad vs Good Indicators ▶

Bad: "The community feels more connected." (No quantity, no who, no timeline, no measurement method.)

Better: "At least 60% of surveyed regular visitors (n ≥ 80) report knowing the name of at least one person they met at the space, within 12 months of opening." (Specific, measurable, time-bound, connected to the outcome.)

Quality Assurance

Three Tests for Your ToC

Before you present your Theory of Change, subject it to three quality tests.

Plausible

Does the causal logic hold? Is there evidence that each outcome leads to the next? Would a sceptic accept the chain?

Feasible

Can you actually do this? Do you have the resources, access, partnerships, and time? Is the scope realistic?

Testable

Can you measure it? Are your indicators specific enough to tell you if the ToC is working - or failing?

A ToC that fails any one of these tests needs rework. Plausibility without feasibility is academic. Feasibility without testability is faith.

Interactive Exercise

Quality Check

For each scenario, identify which quality test it fails.

"Our ToC assumes that building an aesthetically beautiful space will automatically attract users from every demographic group in Bodo."
No causal logic connects aesthetics to demographic diversity. The link is asserted, not argued. This fails the plausibility test.
"We plan to conduct monthly in-depth interviews with every visitor to the space for two years, building a longitudinal dataset of social connection."
The measurement approach is sound in theory, but the scale is unrealistic. Monthly interviews with every visitor for two years requires resources far beyond the project scope. This fails feasibility.
"Our key indicator is that the community space will foster a sense of togetherness and social cohesion among visitors."
"Togetherness" and "social cohesion" are not measurable as stated. No quantity, no threshold, no method, no timeline. This fails the testability test.

Application

Your Challenge

Apply Theory of Change to the Bodo stadium project. Your client wants a community third place adjacent to the new stadium. Your ToC must answer:

1. What is your long-term outcome? Where is your accountability ceiling?

2. What intermediate and early outcomes must occur? Map backwards.

3. What interventions will you propose to the client?

4. What are the assumptions at each link? What evidence supports them?

5. What indicators will you set? Use the "how many of who by when" framework.

6. Is your ToC plausible, feasible, and testable?

Key Takeaways

  • ToC works backwards from impact, not forwards from activities
  • Every causal link carries assumptions - make them explicit and evidence them
  • The accountability ceiling bounds what you can credibly claim
  • Indicators answer: how many of who will reach what level by when
  • A good ToC is plausible, feasible, and testable
