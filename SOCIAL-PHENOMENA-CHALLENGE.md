# Social Phenomena Challenge

## Course Overview

The Social Phenomena Challenge is a 15-week undergraduate course on the BSc Psychology, Economics, and Politics (PEP) programme at Hult International Business School, London. It is the first challenge course on the degree and serves as the model for how challenge courses operate across the programme.

### The Challenge Course Format

Challenge courses are the integrative spine of the PEP degree. Where standard taught modules deliver disciplinary content (microeconomics, political theory, psychology of mind and brain), challenge courses run in parallel and force students to use that content on a live problem with a real external client. The client relationship is not cosmetic — the course culminates in a presentation to the client, and the quality of student work is shaped by the constraint that someone outside the university will act on it.

Each challenge course is organised around a single social phenomenon rather than a syllabus of topics. The phenomenon is the curriculum: every session exists to build the students' capacity to analyse and ultimately intervene in it. This means the course does not survey a field — it goes deep on one case and uses that depth to develop transferable analytical skills.

### Pedagogical Rationale

The course is structured around a strict verb sequence — describe, explain, adjudicate, intervene — that enforces a particular intellectual discipline. Students must characterise a phenomenon empirically before they are permitted to explain it, must hold competing explanations in tension before they are permitted to judge between them, and must have adjudicated before they are permitted to propose action. This sequencing exists because the most common failure mode in applied interdisciplinary work is premature intervention: jumping to solutions before the problem is adequately understood, or allowing a preferred explanation to smuggle in its implied policy response unchallenged.

The interdisciplinary integration is similarly deliberate. Students do not study psychology, economics, and politics in isolation — they apply all three to the same phenomenon, learning where each lens has explanatory power, where it falls short, and how to adjudicate between competing accounts that operate at different levels of analysis. The goal is not three parallel essays stapled together but integrated reasoning that moves between levels of explanation. Because students are concurrently taking their first disciplinary modules, the challenge course gives them an immediate context in which to apply and stress-test concepts they are encountering for the first time elsewhere.

This iteration's phenomenon is the **third place** (Oldenburg) and its decline in the West.

### Learning Outcomes

Students will be able to:

- Describe a social phenomenon using empirical evidence, distinguishing observable features from causal claims
- Explain the phenomenon through psychological, economic, and political frameworks, identifying where accounts converge, conflict, or operate at different levels
- Adjudicate between competing explanations by evaluating evidence quality, scope conditions, and disciplinary assumptions
- Design an evidence-informed intervention using Theory of Change methodology, translating analysis into a concrete proposal for a real client

## Client Assignment

The client is a professional football club in Bodø, Northern Norway. The club is developing a new stadium and wants to incorporate a third place for the surrounding community — a space that promotes community, belonging, and social good. Students pitch what this third place should be and how to make it successful, drawing on everything the course has built across the describe–explain–adjudicate phases.

Key constraint: students will not have access to local community members or end users in Norway.

## Course Structure

15 sessions of 1.5 hours each, organised around four verbs in strict sequential order:

| Phase | Weeks | Verb |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | Introduction / client challenge |
| 2 | 2–3 | **Describe** |
| 3 | 4–5 | **Explain** |
| 4 | 6–7 | **Adjudicate** |
| — | 8 | Reading week |
| 5 | 9–14 | **Intervene** (deep dive into methodologies) |
| 6 | 15 | Client presentation |

The verb ordering is a locked design decision: explanation must not slide into intervention.

## Analytical Spine

"Decline" is decomposed into four separable claims — **supply**, **use**, **quality**, **outcome** — analysed across the three disciplinary lenses. This decomposition is the analytical backbone of the course.

## Intervention Methodology

**Theory of Change** (backward-mapping) is the primary methodology for the intervene phase (weeks 9–14). It is introduced by name in week 1.

## Standing Artefacts

The course uses a small number of longitudinal artefacts that run across sessions to make belief revision and analytical development visible over time. The specific artefacts are TBC — candidates include a claim wall, assumption register, forecast log, and steel-man file.

## Interactive Tools

All interactive classroom tools are built as **single self-contained HTML files** — no external dependencies, no build step. These are the primary artefacts in this repository.

## Parallel Courses

Students concurrently take microeconomics, introduction to political theory and ideology, and genes, mind, brain and behaviour. The week 10 assignment ("Reverse Import") requires students to take a claim from one of these parallel courses and stress-test it against the third-place case.
